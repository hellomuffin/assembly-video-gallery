What changed?

  • Appearance: a continuous skin surface, a shaped palm and thumb base, separate fingers, and fingernails, deformed by the same recorded anatomical joints.
  • Mechanics: separate forearm rotation, wrist bending, thumb joints, and finger joints. Finite actuator strength, anatomical joint limits, inter-finger collisions, and frictional tool contact.
  • Turning: short forearm strokes, finger opening, clearance, reset, and regrasp. Tool motion comes from contact forces; it is not animated to follow the hand.

Why could the original human trajectory be made so quickly?

  • The original full-stool controller commanded two hand poses: position + orientation for each hand, or 12 continuous pose coordinates, plus events such as grasp, release, fit, and drive.
  • Fastener driving had another shortcut: the script directly commanded the screw’s position, orientation, and spin rate. The visible hand did not generate that screw motion through tool contact.
  • The body was fitted afterward. Shoulder, elbow, torso, and leg poses were calculated from the hand paths. Their visual complexity did not represent independently controlled human joints.
  • The task was heavily structured. The part identities, target assembly poses, and operation order were already known. Interpolated waypoints, feedback control, grasp welds, and idealized fastener connections did much of the work. There was no learned human policy, perception problem, or muscle-level optimization.
  • This hand test has 26 continuous commands: 23 anatomical joint-angle targets and 3 carriage translations. A small grasp template and a stage controller generate those commands. The turning grasp was found by searching seven coordinated finger-pose parameters in short physics rollouts.
  • High-dimensional motion can have a low-dimensional controller. Many joints move together. More visible joints does not, by itself, mean a harder control problem than a dexterous robot.

What this does—and does not—establish

  • The lift test must maintain contact and clearance under gravity, then finish with zero hand–tool contacts. The turn test must advance the tool through four bounded strokes, with contact-free resets.
  • The joints use torque-limited position servos. The source anatomy has muscle actuators, but this experiment does not use a muscle controller.
  • The soft contact solver permits small penetrations and small joint-limit excursions; the measurements are reported below. The skin is a visual surface over rigid contact shapes, not simulated deformable tissue.
  • These are nominal-condition tests, not proof of human realism or a closed sim-to-real gap. Whole-arm dynamics, full-body balance, exact screw/thread mechanics, and replacement of the full assembly’s grasp connectors remain to be validated.
