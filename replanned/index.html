How the planning works

  • Choose a feasible order. A scripted task graph builds the frame upright in a fixture, prepares the step and dowels on a support, inserts the second side, fastens the frame, and attaches the top.
  • Search for a grasp that works at both ends. Candidate palm directions, forearm rotation, wrist bend, and elbow bend are scored for pickup and placement. Floor clearance and an approach from outside the furniture matter as well as reaching the part.
  • Move through clearance waypoints. The carry path clears the frame before lower rails enter through its open side. Collision queries identify a narrow insertion path for the step; feedback corrects pose errors along that path. The second side approaches below its joint and rises into place. A failed fit stops the run.
  • Use bounded tool motions. Short strokes alternate with ratchet returns. A compact offset driver provides access beneath the step and top. The screw follows an idealized bore and thread-advance controller; tool–screw contact mechanics are not resolved.
  • Revise failed motions from saved states. During development, grasp choices, clearance routes, and insertion tracking were revised after failed tests. Checked continuations preserve the part poses and velocities from the last accepted assembly state. The planner is task-specific and uses known CAD assembly poses.
  • Check the released result. Completion requires all parts to be connected and aligned, all fasteners recorded, both hands released, four foot contacts, and a stable upright stool after carrying it off the workholding supports onto the floor.

Why can such a detailed trajectory be generated quickly?

  • The motion is structured. Known CAD poses, an operation graph, grasp templates, and a small candidate search replace an unconstrained search over every human joint at every instant.
  • The hand interface has 58 continuous pose coordinates: two six-dimensional forearm pose targets plus 23 joint-angle targets for each hand. Grasp, release, fixture, and driving modes are discrete events. The screw controller adds its own pose and spin targets.
  • The whole body is not an independently controlled dynamic human. Its posture is calculated around the active hand and finite limb lengths. Visual complexity is not evidence of a learned human policy or muscle-level planning.
  • Timing: hand commands update at 50 Hz and forearm control at 500 Hz. Physics integration was refined from 500 to 1,000 Hz during checked continuations; replay states are recorded at 25 Hz.
